# Off My High Horse

> I've stopped trying to compete with AI. It's a relief.

- Author: Joel Hägvall
- Published: 2026-10-25
- Tags: thoughts, ai

It's such a relief to finally admit it: I'm mostly an agent orchestrator now.

I don't write most of my code anymore. I describe what I want, split it into pieces, hand them to agents, review what comes back and decide what ships. My job is direction and judgement. The typing is done by something else.

## The starting assumption

I go into everything with one assumption: AI will be able to do everything we humans do on computers. Not some of it. All of it.

That means jobs will disappear. Mine included. In [Automate Everything Possible](/blog/automate-everything) I wrote that nobody is safe. I meant it, and that includes me.

## I will never outperform it

I like this work. I like building, and I like the moment when something finally clicks. But I'm never going to outperform an agent. Not on speed, not on breadth, not at three in the morning.

Once you accept that, the stress goes away. There's no race to lose if you stop running it.

## Unhooking my identity

So I've disconnected my identity from my profession. I used to be "a developer". Now I'm someone who likes to build, for as long as building needs me.

If it doesn't, I'll find something else to do. Farming, maybe. Something with my hands, outside, where the feedback loop is a season instead of a build. And I'm fine with that.

## Rust where it shines

I like DHH, the creator of Ruby on Rails. At Rails World this year he said Rust is amazing if you never, ever, ever have to look at it yourself. That's the person who built a whole framework around developer happiness, telling you to use a language he doesn't want to read.

And he's right. You can think Rust is ugly. You can barely understand it. It doesn't matter anymore. Agents love it, the compiler catches their mistakes, and the performance is insane. So why not use it where it shines?

That thinking has to be applied everywhere. Pick the right tool for the job, not the one you happen to know. That's how we get so much further, and how we stop holding on to "I only know Java".

Because what a boring world that would be to live in.

## Off the high horse

For a long time there was a quiet pride in being the person who could make the computer do things. The one people called when nothing worked. That's the high horse, and I've climbed down from it.

It's calmer down here. I don't need to defend a title or a skill set. I use the best tools there are, I build what's worth building, and when the tools don't need me anymore, I'll go grow something.
